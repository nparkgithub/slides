An introduction to Paul’s letter

Romans

The gospel: God’s righteousness
revealed, received, and lived.

by Daniel Park

- Welcome everyone and explain that this is an orientation session. We are not trying to answer every question in Romans today; we are gaining a map for reading the letter well. - Romans is both carefully reasoned theology and a real pastoral letter. Paul writes to believers he has not yet met because he wants to strengthen them and partner with them in mission. - The central claim of the letter is that God’s righteousness is revealed in the gospel. That righteousness is not achieved by human effort; it is received through faith in Christ. - Invite listeners to keep three movements in mind: our need, God’s gift, and the transformed life that follows. - The aim is not merely to become more informed about Romans, but to become more humble, hopeful, and faithful as the gospel takes root in us.

The big idea

Why Romans matters

Romans is Paul’s fullest, most sustained explanation of the gospel—and of how God makes sinners right with himself.
  • It begins with the universal problem: all stand accountable before God.
  • It announces God’s gift: righteousness through faith in Christ.
  • It traces the gospel into a transformed life, a reconciled people, and a renewed hope.
- Read the quotation slowly. Romans is Paul’s fullest sustained explanation of the gospel, but it never treats the gospel as a merely abstract idea. - On the first point, emphasize the honesty of Romans: it begins with the universal problem of sin. Paul levels every human claim to superiority, whether religious, moral, or cultural. - On the second point, emphasize gift. God does not ignore evil; he acts in Christ so that he remains just while justifying the ungodly. - On the third point, show the destination of the letter. Grace produces a new way of life, a reconciled people, and a durable hope in the midst of suffering. - The governing question is not simply, “How can people be better?” but, “How can God be just and justify the ungodly?” Romans answers that question through Jesus Christ.

Writer & date

Paul writes from Corinth, c. A.D. 56–57

  • Writer: Paul identifies himself at once (1:1).
  • Place: likely Corinth, during the winter.
  • Courier: Phoebe, commended in 16:1–2.
  • Moment: near the close of Paul’s third missionary journey.

- Paul introduces himself in Romans 1:1, but the setting adds important color. He writes from Corinth, likely during the winter of A.D. 56–57, near the close of his third missionary journey. - He is not writing from Rome and has not yet visited the Roman believers. That makes the letter unusually deliberate: he must introduce his gospel and his ministry through writing. - Point out the timeline. Paul’s ministry has already moved from Damascus through several missionary journeys; Romans comes at a mature and consequential moment. - His immediate concern is a collection for needy believers in Jerusalem. His future hope is to travel through Rome and continue west toward Spain. - This setting helps us hear Romans as a letter written between completed work and an uncertain future, with mission, danger, fellowship, and trust in God all in view.

Writer & date — geographic setting

Paul’s world: from Corinth toward Rome

Read the map with the letter

  • Corinth: the likely place of writing.
  • Jerusalem: Paul’s immediate destination with a collection for the saints.
  • Rome: a church Paul longs to visit and strengthen.
  • Spain: beyond the edge of this map—and the next frontier Paul hopes to reach.
- Use the map to make the geography concrete. Corinth is the likely place of writing; Jerusalem is Paul’s immediate destination; Rome is the church he longs to visit; Spain is the future horizon beyond the map. - Notice the surprising direction of travel. Paul is about to go east to Jerusalem, not west to Rome. Yet Rome already occupies his prayers and plans. - Paul does not treat the Roman church simply as a stopover. He wants mutual encouragement, fellowship, and partnership in taking the gospel farther west. - The map reminds us that Romans was born out of movement and mission. Theology in this letter serves worship, unity, courage, and gospel advance. - Transition by asking: what kind of church was Paul writing to, and why did its composition matter so much?

Recipients

A church Paul had not yet visited

Known for faith

“Your faith is proclaimed in all the world” (1:8).

Many house churches

Chapter 16 pictures connected communities, not one centralized congregation.

Jew & Gentile

The gospel creates one family from people with different histories and instincts.

The pastoral pressure point: How can diverse believers live as one people under the same Lord?

- Paul did not found this church. It was likely formed by early Jewish and Gentile believers, perhaps including people connected with Pentecost and later travel between Rome and the eastern Mediterranean. - The first card highlights their public reputation: Paul thanks God because their faith is known throughout the world. Begin with encouragement, not deficiency. - The second card reminds us that this was probably a network of house churches, not one centralized gathering. That means unity had to be practiced across several communities. - The third card identifies a major pastoral challenge: Jewish and Gentile believers brought different histories, customs, and instincts into one family in Christ. - This tension helps explain why Romans 9–11 and 14–15 matter so much. The gospel does not erase every difference, but it creates one people who welcome one another under the same Lord.

Why write?

Three purposes, one gospel

Missionary

Prepare for a visit and seek partnership for ministry farther west—toward Spain (15:22–24).

Pastoral

Strengthen a healthy church amid pressures, disagreements, and false teaching.

Apologetic

Set out a careful, durable account of the gospel Paul proclaimed.

- These three purposes belong together rather than competing with one another. - Missionary: Paul hopes that Rome will become a place of fellowship and support as he takes the gospel to places where Christ has not yet been named, especially toward Spain. - Pastoral: a healthy church still needs strengthening. Romans addresses divisions, conscience questions, suffering, obedience, and life together. - Apologetic or explanatory: Paul gives a careful, durable account of the gospel he preaches so the church can recognize truth and resist distortion. - Paul wants Rome to understand the gospel deeply enough to live it together and send it onward. Good doctrine serves love, mission, and perseverance.

The theme

“The righteousness of God”

“For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes … For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from first to last.”
Romans 1:16–17
  • God is righteous: holy, faithful, and just.
  • God gives righteousness: received by faith, not earned by performance.
  • God forms a righteous people: the gospel bears fruit in embodied obedience.
- Treat Romans 1:16–17 as the thesis statement for the whole letter. Paul is not ashamed because the gospel is God’s power, not merely good advice. - Explain “righteousness of God” carefully. It includes God’s own holy and faithful character, his just action against sin, and the right standing he gives to those who trust Christ. - The first point keeps God at the center: he is holy, faithful, and just. The gospel does not lower God’s standards. - The second point keeps grace at the center: righteousness is received by faith, not achieved by performance, heritage, or moral comparison. - The third point keeps transformation at the center: the gift of righteousness creates a people whose embodied obedience displays God’s mercy in the world.

The message does not end with forgiveness; it moves toward a renewed people who offer themselves to God.

- Walk through the arc one section at a time. Romans 1:1–17 introduces Paul, his gospel, and the letter’s central theme. - Romans 1:18–3:20 gives the diagnosis: Gentile and Jew alike stand under sin. Paul removes every basis for boasting before he announces grace. - Romans 3:21–8:39 announces rescue and assurance: justification by faith, union with Christ, freedom from sin’s dominion, life in the Spirit, and hope in suffering. - Chapters 9–11 are not a detour. They ask whether God’s promises can be trusted in light of Israel’s unbelief, and they display God’s faithfulness and mercy. - Chapters 12–16 show the lived result: worship, love, humility, submission, welcome, and mission. The letter moves from what God has done to what a renewed people become.

Christian ethics grows from grace; it does not compete with grace.

How to read Romans

Read the argument—and receive the invitation

  • Follow the therefores: Paul builds his case carefully.
  • Keep the whole letter in view: grace and obedience belong together.
  • Listen for the community: the gospel reshapes how believers welcome one another.
  • Let the doxology set the horizon: “to the only wise God be glory forevermore” (16:27).
- Romans can feel dense because Paul builds an argument over many chapters. Encourage the group to read slowly and pay attention to connecting words such as “therefore,” “for,” “but now,” and “so that.” - Keep the whole letter in view. We should not isolate justification from sanctification, grace from obedience, or personal faith from the church’s shared life. - Listen for the community dimension. Paul repeatedly teaches believers how to welcome one another, use freedom in love, and live humbly across differences. - Let the doxology set the horizon. The goal of careful study is worship: glory to the only wise God through Jesus Christ. - Invite a simple prayerful posture: ask God not only to help us understand Romans, but to let Romans deepen faith, humility, hope, and love.

Romans 1:16–17

The gospel is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes.

Where we begin

Next: Romans 1:1–7 — the gospel promised beforehand

- Pause here and let Romans 1:16–17 settle in. This is the banner over the entire study: the gospel is God’s power for salvation to everyone who believes. - Emphasize that Paul is not ashamed because the gospel accomplishes what human effort and religious achievement cannot accomplish. - Invite the group to listen for three recurring words and ideas throughout the letter: God’s power, God’s righteousness, and faith. - Salvation is not narrowed to escape from judgment; it includes God’s rescuing and restoring work, creating a people who live under his mercy. - Transition to Romans 1:1–7. Paul begins not with the Roman believers’ situation, but with his identity as a servant of Christ and the gospel promised beforehand.

Takeaways

Romans: the gospel at the center

  • Saved by Grace Through Faith: Salvation comes entirely by faith through God's grace, not by personal performance or good deeds.
  • Ongoing Struggle with Sin: Being saved does not make us immune to daily battles with recurring sin and human weakness.
  • Purpose in Suffering: Enduring trials builds perseverance, refined character, and an unshakable hope.
  • Inseparable Love of God: Absolutely nothing—including personal failures, missteps, or sin—can separate us from God's love.

Carry this with you: Romans brings us from our need, to God’s gift, to a life remade by grace.

Core message: God gives righteousness through Christ, received by faith, producing a changed life.

Diamond-ring analogy adapted from a traditional illustration attributed to Philipp Jakob Spener: Scripture as a ring, Romans as its precious stone, and Romans 8 as its sparkle.

- Introduce the ring-and-diamond illustration as a helpful picture, not a ranking system for Scripture. Every part of God’s Word is valuable; the image highlights Romans as an especially concentrated presentation of the gospel. - As the first panel appears, explain the image: Scripture is the ring, Romans is a precious stone within it, and Romans 8 is often treasured as a particularly bright expression of gospel assurance. - As the summary list appears, name the major takeaways: salvation is by grace through faith; believers still face a real struggle with sin; suffering is not meaningless; and nothing can separate God’s people from his love in Christ. - As the historical voices appear, use them briefly. Romans has repeatedly clarified the gospel for believers across centuries, including Augustine, Luther, Calvin, and Stott. - End with the core message. We cannot establish our own righteousness. God provides righteousness through Jesus Christ; faith receives it; and grace transforms how we live. - This is a good place to invite reflection: which part of Romans’ message most challenges or comforts you as we begin?
